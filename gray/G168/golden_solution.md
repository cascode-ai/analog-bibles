# Golden Solution - G168

## Reference answer

Valid reasons (any three, each with its explanation):
1. Higher transconductance: electron mobility is about 2–3× hole mobility, so for the same current and $W/L$ the n-channel pair has larger $g_{m1}$ ($\propto\sqrt{\mu}$). That raises the first-stage gain $g_{m1}(r_{o2}\parallel r_{o4})$ and the gain-bandwidth product $g_{m1}/C_C$. Alternatively, the same $g_m$ comes from smaller devices with less capacitance, for higher speed.
2. Lower input offset from the load: the mirror-mismatch contribution is $(g_{m3}/g_{m1})\Delta V_{t3,4}$. With an n-input pair and a p-channel mirror the ratio is $\sqrt{\mu_p/\mu_n} < 1$ (instead of $\sqrt{\mu_n/\mu_p} > 1$), roughly 3× lower for equal sizes and currents.
3. Lower input-referred thermal (white) noise for a given bias current and the same $W/L$ (strong inversion), because thermal noise voltage $\propto 1/\sqrt{g_m}$ and the n-channel pair has the larger $g_m$; equivalently, a given white-noise target with smaller input devices or less current.
4. Common-mode input range shifted toward the positive supply: an n-input stage works for $V_{IC}$ from about $-V_{SS} + V_t + 2V_{ov}$ up to (and with body effect possibly beyond) $V_{DD} - |V_{GS3}| + V_t$. Choose it when the input signals sit near $V_{DD}$ (e.g. high-side sensing).
5. Process/well considerations: in a p-well process only the n-channel devices can sit in their own well with source tied to body. This removes body effect and the supply-capacitance coupling through the body (better PSRR in switched-capacitor circuits), so in that process the n-channel input is the one that gets these benefits. (In a triple-well process both polarities can be isolated, so this is not an exclusive n-channel advantage there.)
6. Power-supply rejection: in the conventional complementary connection, the supply on which the tail source and second-stage load are referenced gets the cancellation (very high low-frequency PSRR, given matched and properly ratioed biasing); with n inputs this is the negative supply. Choose it if that supply is the noisier one.
(A known drawback, not a reason: p-channel devices usually have lower 1/f noise, and in the conventional direct-coupled topology the n-input first-stage output sits at $V_{DD} - |V_{GS3}|$, matching the $V_{SG}$ of a p-channel driver, so the second-stage driver becomes a lower-$g_m$ p-channel device, which lowers the nondominant pole. Conversely a p-input pair with an n-channel mirror naturally drives an n-channel second stage.)

## Grading notes

+2: at least three distinct, correct reasons with explanations, e.g. higher $g_m$/gain/GBW from higher electron mobility, lower mirror-induced offset, lower thermal noise, common-mode range near $V_{DD}$, well/body-effect or PSRR considerations. +1: three reasons listed but one weakly explained, or two well-explained reasons plus one marginal. 0: only one or two valid reasons; or at least three valid reasons plus one further reason that is false and asserted as fact (one of the false reasons below). -2 (asserted as fact): false reasons that make up most of the answer or leave fewer than two valid reasons, such as "n-channel devices have lower 1/f noise", "n-channel input pairs give a common-mode range that includes the negative supply", or "an n-channel input naturally gives (or a p-channel input forces) the opposite-polarity-to-conventional second stage, so the n-input op amp has a higher-$g_m$ n-channel driver" (in the conventional direct-coupled design the n-input stage drives a p-channel driver whose $V_{SG}$ matches the mirror; a p-input stage drives an n-channel driver). An answer that explicitly assumes a different, self-consistently biased second stage (e.g. a level shift) is judged on that circuit; only conclusions that hold for it count. Second-stage and PSRR reasons apply to the conventional complementary connection.
