# Golden Solution - A046

## Reference answer

Layout (e) is the best: three identical unit resistors with R (the bar connected to A) in the centre and 2R formed by the two outer bars in series, so the arrangement is unit-matched, common-centroid, all bars have the same orientation, and dummy bars at both ends give every active bar the same surroundings.

- (a): six units in series (R = the two inner vertical bars, 2R = the two outer vertical bars plus the top and bottom horizontal bars). It is roughly common-centroid, but 2R contains horizontal and vertical bars while R is all vertical: different orientation (not photolithographic invariant: etch, lithography and stress differ with direction), the inner and outer bars have different surroundings, there are no dummies, and the long, unequal metal routing adds series interconnect resistance that is not ratioed (the contact count itself, 8 on the 2R side versus 4 on the R side, does scale 2:1).
- (b): three identical parallel bars in series with R at one end (A) and 2R the other two: unit-matched and same orientation, but not common-centroid (a linear gradient changes R relative to 2R), and no dummies (the outer bars see different neighbours from the middle one).
- (c): has dummy bars and the same orientation, but the order is still sequential (R at one side, 2R on the other) so it is not common-centroid; the dog-bone bars with narrow bodies make the width (edge-variation) sensitivity larger.
- (d): same connection as (e) (R in the centre, 2R = the two outer bars in series, common centroid, same orientation), but without dummies: the outer bars (2R) see a different environment (no neighbour on one side) from the centre bar (R), a systematic error. It cancels a linear gradient, but its edge error is not cancelled: with edge bars $r(1+\varepsilon)$ and an interior bar $r$, (d) gives $2(1+\varepsilon)$ whereas (b) gives $(2+\varepsilon)/(1+\varepsilon)\approx 2-\varepsilon$, so whether (d) beats (b) or (c) depends on whether gradients or edge effects dominate.
- (f): a single strip with a tap: the end contacts and their silicide/contact transitions are in the current path and do not scale 2:1 ($R_{B-tap} = 2xR_\square/W + R_c$ versus $xR_\square/W + R_c$), the tap contact region on the strip disturbs the current path and its exact position, and the two sections have different centroids, so a gradient along the strip directly changes the ratio.

## Grading notes

+2: picks (e) and justifies it by unit matching, common centroid (R between the two halves of 2R), same orientation and dummies; gives a correct reason against each of (a), (b), (c), (d) and (f) (verdicts: (a) mixed orientation; (b) no common centroid, no dummies; (c) no common centroid, narrow bodies; (d) no dummies; (f) contact/end resistance not ratioed and gradient sensitivity). +1: (e) chosen with the right main reasons, with one or two of the other options dismissed for a wrong or missing reason. 0: (d) chosen with correct common-centroid reasoning (dummies overlooked), or (e) chosen with no valid explanation. −2 (asserted as fact): (a), (b), (c) or (f) chosen as the best, or misreadings of the connections such as R placed on an outer bar in (d)/(e). All six layouts are nominally 2:1 for ideal identical units; an answer that says so, notes that (f) is exactly 2:1 in the ideal uniform-strip model, and still names (e) as the best for a precise ratio with the reasons above is not docked for that qualification. Choosing (f) outright as the best (dismissing its unratioed end contacts and gradient sensitivity) remains −2. No ranking among (b), (c) and (d) is required; the first-order statement that the edge error of (d) is about twice that of (b) is correct.
