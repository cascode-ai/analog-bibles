# Golden Solution - R705

## Reference answer

Violating a rule leaves the geometry outside the margin that fabrication tolerances need, so the likely results are shorts, opens, leakage or ill-defined devices:
- $A_1$ (active–active spacing, between the drain diffusions of the two input transistors): the n+ regions may merge or leak into each other (isolation fails, punch-through or a parasitic field transistor). The two output nodes are shorted or resistively coupled and the differential gain collapses.
- $A_2$ (metal width, the line bringing an input to its gate): the line may be broken or necked (open circuit, high resistance, electromigration failure). The input transistor's gate floats or is poorly connected, so the amplifier does not work.
- $A_3$ (metal–metal spacing, the tail-gate bias line next to the ground/source line): the two lines may short. The tail gate is tied to ground, the tail current vanishes and the amplifier turns off (or its bias is wrong). At best the coupling capacitance grows.
- $A_4$ (enclosure of contact by active, at the tail device's contact): the contact may fall partly off the diffusion onto the junction edge or field region. The contact resistance becomes high or unreliable (in the limit open), which raises the tail source potential and reduces or removes the tail current; the metal may also reach the p-substrate at the junction edge. For the tail source contact marked in the figure this substrate contact is electrically harmless (source and substrate are both at ground), at most adding leakage; if $A_4$ is read as a drain contact (e.g. the tail drain), the same short would clamp that node to ground and bypass the tail current source.
- $A_5$ (poly–active spacing, between an input transistor's active area and the poly that brings its gate out): with misalignment the poly can land on the active area. It then blocks the S/D implant locally, forms an extra, ill-defined gate region or parasitic device, and adds gate–drain/source capacitance. The two input devices no longer match (offset), and the circuit may fail if a parasitic channel forms.
- $A_6$ (active–well spacing, NMOS drain to n-well edge): the n+ drain can touch or punch through to the n-well, which sits at $V_{DD}$. The output leaks or is shorted to $V_{DD}$, and latch-up susceptibility increases.
- $A_7$ (enclosure of active by well, PMOS active inside the n-well): the p+ source/drain can extend past the well edge into the p-substrate. The PMOS source ($V_{DD}$) or drain (output) is then shorted to the grounded substrate: a supply short or a dead output.
- $A_8$ (poly–poly spacing, between the gate ends of the two input transistors): the two gates may short. The two inputs are tied together, the differential input is lost and the driving stages are shorted to each other.

## Grading notes

Eight parts of equal weight; each needs a physically correct consequence (short/open/leakage/ill-defined device) tied to the node involved. +2: all eight right. +1: six or seven right, the rest missing or vague. 0: about four or five right. -1: two or three right. -2 (asserted as fact): most consequences wrong, or a critical misreading such as treating the shorting rules ($A_1$, $A_3$, $A_6$, $A_7$, $A_8$) as merely costing area or as harmless, or claiming that a violation improves performance (e.g., closer gates reducing offset). Reasonable variants of which nodes are involved (e.g., $A_4$ read as the tail drain contact) are acceptable when the stated physical effect is correct; for $A_4$ the high-resistance/open contact is the main consequence, and a metal-to-substrate short at the grounded tail source is not by itself a circuit failure. For $A_5$, poly over active is insulated by the gate oxide, so a direct gate-to-diffusion short is not the mechanism; an answer that asserts such a short but also gives the implant-blocking/parasitic-device consequence counts that part as half right (a minor false claim, not a cap on the whole score), e.g. seven parts right plus a half-right $A_5$ earns +1.
