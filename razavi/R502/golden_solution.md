# Golden Solution - R502

## Reference answer

Across $M_1$ the capacitor sits between the highest-impedance node (the input, ≈ $r_{O3}$ = 1 MΩ) and a node driven with large inverting gain ($g_{m1}r_{O1}$ ≈ 52). It is Miller-multiplied there, creating the dominant pole with a small capacitor, and it splits the poles. The other two placements do not have these properties:
- Across $M_2$ (drain of $M_1$ to output): the Miller multiplication now acts at the drain of $M_1$, whose resistance is only $r_{O1}$ ≈ 10 kΩ (100× lower than the input node), with a gain of only $g_{m2}r_{O2}$ ≈ 14. The natural low-frequency pole at the input node (≈ 9×10⁵ rad/s, from $C_{GS1}$ and the Miller-multiplied $C_{GD1}$ with 1 MΩ) is not split away, so the loop keeps two low-frequency poles. Worse, the right-half-plane zero $g_{m2}/C_C$ always lies only $g_{m2}r_{O1}\cdot g_{m2}r_{O2} \approx 380$ times above the new dominant pole $1/(r_{O1}g_{m2}r_{O2}C_C)$, where the loop gain is still ≈ $T_0/380 \approx 300$. Above $g_{m2}/C_C$ the capacitor makes $M_2$ act as a diode-connected device, and the remaining loop through $M_3$ and $M_1$ (two inversions) is positive feedback with gain ≈ $g_{m3}r_{O3}\cdot g_{m1}/g_{m2} \approx 300 > 1$. No value of $C_C$ stabilizes this placement: a right-half-plane closed-loop pole remains for any $C_C$ (checked numerically from 1 pF to 1 µF). For a capacitor across $M_1$ the same residual loop, $g_{m2}r_{O2}\cdot g_{m3}/g_{m1} \approx 0.44$, is below 1.
- Across $M_3$ (output to input node): this capacitor closes a path from the output back to the input around the forward amplifier input → $M_1$ → $M_2$ → output, which is non-inverting (two inverting stages, gain ≈ +700). A capacitor around a non-inverting gain gives positive feedback: its Miller capacitance at the input is $(1 - A)C_C < 0$, and the capacitive path raises the feedback at high frequencies instead of rolling the loop gain off. It destabilizes the loop rather than compensating it.
